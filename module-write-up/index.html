Conscious Discipline at Home

Conscious Discipline at Home is a free, self-paced eLearning module that introduces parents to Conscious Discipline, a framework for de-escalation and behavioral development used by many schools. As a parent of a child at a CD school, I know personally how important this framework is, but we as parents don’t typically receive any formal training. I made this module to help us understand what our kids’ school is teaching and give us a safe way to practice it so we can reinforce the method at home.

Structure

The module comes in three parts, with each aligned to a specific learning objective I developed for the project:

1

Learn

Introduces Conscious Discipline concepts in the order a parent would need them during a tough situation. After a bit of scientific background, the parent learns how to assess their own brain state in the moment, regain composure, assess their child’s brain state, and choose what helps the child at each state. Each part includes mini-assessments to test knowledge along the way.

2

Bring It Together

Rather than throw the parent straight into a role-play scenario, this section offers two real-life situations and guides the parent, step by step, through the processes learned in the Learn section. At each step, the parent receives feedback from a “Coach” character.

3

Try It Out

Three branching role-play scenarios that allow a parent to have a back-and-forth “talk” with a 5-year-old child character who is in a tough moment. The child responds based on how well the parent is implementing the CD methodology, the Coach gives feedback and suggestions for phrasing if needed, and each scenario comes with a summary screen highlighting where they were strong and where they could improve. No scores given.

Learning Design Highlights

No one likes to be told how to parent. This goes for many instructional design situations; we often encounter learners who are reluctant to be there. Parenting is a complex subject with no easy answers, but complex subjects are my specialty. That’s why this module needed an experienced designer:

Sensitivity to the learners

A complex subject needs complex assessments, which meant allowing the parents to type out what they’d actually say. Feedback stays aligned to the methodology, and comes in the form of “Strong”, “Nearly there”, and “Not yet”, rather than numerical scores, so a parent never feels their parenting itself is being judged.

Safety checks

Some parents will use the open-ended text entry to vent, and some may honestly say something dangerous. So every answer must first pass a safety check. If it’s triggered, nothing is scored or saved, and the parent sees a message about how to get help.

Adult learning theory

Design decisions are backed by theory. The learning objectives were crafted using Mager’s three-part objectives with an eye toward pushing the learner further up Bloom’s Taxonomy. Gagné’s Nine Events and Knowles’ andragogy principles helped shape the structure. A few examples:

Gagné: Gain attention
Opens with a “hook” question
Knowles: Experience
Coach feedback never assumes parental ignorance, always offers encouragement, not judgment
Gagné: Provide learning guidance
In Bring It Together, the Coach leads the parent step by step through a scenario
Knowles: Self-concept
Parents answer in their own words.
Gagné: Enhance retention and transfer
The opening hook comes back as the last role-play scenario, creating a bookend that allows the parent to see how far they’ve come.

UX/UI design

Every micro- and macro-decision was run through the framework of Mayer’s multimedia learning principles, as well as UX and graphic design concepts such as cognitive load, chunking, Hick’s Law, the laws of proximity and continuity, and color theory.

Design Cycle Highlights

My primary framework was ADDIE, with some iterative ideas borrowed from SAM.

Analysis, Objectives, Concept

Began with my own experience as a parent. Then, I planned the module out by reversing Kirkpatrick’s Levels of Evaluation to formulate learning objectives and design concepts: results desired, then what behaviors the module could help change, what learning could help with that, and finally what kinds of interaction and visual design busy parents would find engaging.

Design and Development

Storyboarding with pencil and paper, then Claude Design for wireframing and visual design mockups, coding and debugging; a combination of various LLMs for script drafting and artwork. I edited every line of the script and took the images through days of prompting to get the results I wanted.

Implementation and Evaluation

First user test with me observing revealed numerous flaws in the flow and comprehension. I flagged 113 areas to address. This led to SAM-style cycles of iteration and re-evaluation that vastly improved the module’s design.

Accessibility

Audited using WCAG 2.1 AA/Section 508 standards, on every screen at desktop and phone widths. VoiceOver, multiple passes, on an iPhone and a Mac, for blind and low-vision users. Every animation has a still version for users who need reduced motion settings.

Further Evaluation

The module has been reviewed by two principals from a Conscious Discipline school, and is currently under review by a certified CD instructor. Once the SME review is back, I will implement changes and begin beta testing with 15–20 parents I know personally before opening up the module to the wider school community.

Post-Beta Expansion

Will explore opt-in AI models to make the role-play scenarios more fluid, and to evaluate spoken answers, so the coaching can give feedback not just on wording, but on tone. The current feedback model can’t detect, for example, sarcasm, or when heated words are shouted vs. softly spoken.

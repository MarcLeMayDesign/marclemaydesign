Principle 1
Discipline is teaching, not punishing
When a child misbehaves, it is natural to ask "how do I stop this right now?" But stopping a behavior and teaching a child what to do instead are two different things. When we focus only on ending the moment, we miss the chance to build a skill they can use next time.
The old frame
"How do I stop this behavior right now?"
The shift
"What skill is my child missing, and how can I teach it?"
Key concept
Behavior management stops discomfort in the moment. Instructional discipline builds skills for the future.
INTERACTIVE · PLACEHOLDER
Two framings, one moment
The thrown toy, played out twice — tap a framing to see what the child learns from it.

Principle 2
Look beneath the behavior
When your child melts down, screams, or refuses a task, the behavior is what you see — but it does not tell the whole story. Instead of assuming your child is choosing to be difficult, look beneath the surface. What are they trying to communicate that they cannot yet put into words?
The old frame
"My child is choosing to be difficult and push my buttons."
The shift
"My child is overwhelmed and communicating an unmet need."
Key concept
Behavior is the symptom on the surface. Missing skills and emotional distress are the causes underneath.
INTERACTIVE · PLACEHOLDER
Above and below the line
Screaming and the kicked-off shoe sit above the waterline. Drag it down to find hunger, exhaustion, no words yet.

Principle 3
State affects access to skills
Have you ever tried reasoning with someone who is furious? It does not work. When children are overwhelmed, the brain switches to survival mode and logic is out of reach. Before you can teach a lesson or hold a limit, you have to help them — and yourself — back to a calm state.
The old frame
"I need to fix this and reason with them right now."
The shift
"An upset brain cannot listen or learn. We both need to settle first."
Key concept
You cannot teach a skill into an active storm. And there are two states in the room: if you are dysregulated, your stress escalates theirs.
INTERACTIVE · PLACEHOLDER
Locked, or open
Switch a brain between survival and calm, and watch which sentences can get in.

Principle 4 of 4

Principle 4
Connection before correction
Parents often worry that validating a child's frustration means giving up control. It does not. Connection is not permission — it is the bridge that lets your limit be heard. Acknowledge how they feel first, and the clear, calm boundary lands.
The old frame
"If I validate their feelings, I am giving in and letting them win."
The shift
"I can connect with empathy and still hold the boundary."
Key concept
Understanding is not permission. Connection creates the safety that lets the limit actually land: "You wanted to stay at the park. It is time to go home now."
INTERACTIVE · PLACEHOLDER
Build the two-part sentence
Pick a feeling, then a limit, and hear how the pair sounds out loud. Limit without feeling is offered too.

What the layout commits to. One template, four fills — a parent who learns to read principle 1 reads the other three for free. Left column is the argument in one order every time: what it is, why it matters, the old frame, the shift, the key concept. The interactive sits right, where the screen's one action also lives, and the progress bars sit under it rather than in the header, so the sense of "four of these" belongs to the reading rather than the chrome. Three script elements are gone: Miss Rivera's at-school/at-home bridge (held for a better home than a box on every screen), the narration toggle (Section 1 is read, not narrated — the voiceover copy is now the body text), and the reference drawer on the overview, which the ? already owns. Open: principle 3 carries a longer key concept than the others, because the script's "two states" idea has nowhere else to go — it may want to be the interactive's second half instead.

What changed in 10d: the header rule is gone, radii go back to 14px on the field, the pill and the button, and the score keeps its blue field — it is the one number on the screen and the tint is what makes it findable. Everything else drops its container. "What you said" is now an uppercase label over plain text instead of a tinted block or a left rule, and the two feedback notes are separated by color alone — blue for what worked, clay for what to work on. The quote's left border and the two divider rules are also gone, which leaves exactly one line in the whole screen: none.

More personality in the details — an angled e, a curved leg on the R — without becoming a display face. Holds up best at small sizes, which matters for the question lists.
On the surface question. You leaned soft but wanted crisp — 10a and 10b are the same screen in each, so the choice is visible rather than theoretical. My read: soft groups the debrief's three ideas without adding a single line, which is exactly what the border rule asked for. Crisp is more contemporary but pushes the warmth entirely onto the paper color, and this screen is where a parent is being told what they got wrong. A third option exists if you want it — soft blocks for content, crisp 4px corners on controls.

The problem

My child's school trains its staff in a discipline framework, and sent the parents a one-page PDF. The parents I know read it and agreed with it. But the first time we need any of it, it's 7:40 in the morning, there's a shoe across the hallway, and the bus is coming.

So the module is built around one bet: trying the words is not the same as reading them. A parent who has typed their own sentence and had it answered starts from a different place than a parent who has only read the handout.

Before any screen got styled, a wireframe audit triaged 41 items into settled, blocking, and open; seven blockers closed.

An unscored baseline sits before section 1 and its reflection after section 3, so the comparison is self-referenced: the parent measures against their own first answer, not a model answer and not a number. Attempting the moment before being taught it is also what makes the teaching land.

Inviting honesty means being ready for it

The module asks parents to type what they would really say in a hard moment. Most of the time that produces an answer, and the matcher scores it. Sometimes it produces a disclosure — about their child, their home, or themselves — and a rules-based matcher has no business grading that.

So five pre-written responses sit in front of the scoring. Every free-text field in the module runs the same order: safety check first, then the matcher, then the band, then feedback. If the safety check fires, all the rest is skipped, nothing is scored or recorded, and the parent is given an option to return and try again.

The five
  • A parent frightened of their own anger, or of what they have already done
  • A child being hurt, or hurting someone — the one case with a destination inside the module
  • A child whose safety is past what a practice module should touch
  • A parent in crisis themselves
  • A parent asking for real advice about their real child (the most likely of the five)

Each one acknowledges without assessing, names a single concrete next step rather than a menu, teaches nothing, and hands control back with the parent's answer still in the field. There is no judgment, no flashing red banner. Responses appear in the coach's ordinary slot. 

The interesting failure is not the one you would guess. Missing a disclosure is bad; firing wrongly is worse, because the practice content is itself about a child melting down — "she hit me" is an expected sentence in the sibling scenario. The detection lists inside role-plays are deliberately narrower than elsewhere, and the phrase most likely to over-fire is the one an exhausted parent uses at the end of a long evening. Being careful there means accepting some misses in exchange for never telling a tired parent the module thinks they are a risk.

The fifth response is the one I would point at. A parent asking "what should I do about my son's biting" is not answering the question, and both available failures are real: an answer is impossible, and scoring them as wrong is insulting. Saying plainly that it cannot answer, and naming who can, is the same boundary the disclaimer draws.

What this transfers to

Parenting is the subject matter, not the design problem. The design problem was scoring what someone wrote, in a domain where being told you got it wrong stings.

  • Free-text scenario design. Writing prompts that can't be answered by pattern-matching the last screen, with matcher criteria and per-branch feedback for each.
  • Branching feedback that names criteria, not scores. Including the case where one criterion cancels the other three.
  • Knowing when an assessment should refuse to assess. Any open response field in a sensitive domain — healthcare, HR, safeguarding, compliance — will eventually collect a disclosure. Designing the interception before the scoring is a habit, not a feature.
  • Behavior change over recall. A baseline captured before instruction and shown back after.
  • Help design under load. Separating reference a learner reads once from support they need mid-task, in one tap.
  • Low-stakes assessment in a high-stakes emotional context. Same problem as compliance, feedback, and difficult-conversation training.

How it gets tested

No learner has used this yet. The module goes live in mid-October, and what follows is the plan — written before the results exist, so it can't be written to fit them.

Oct 14–24
Eight parents, unmoderated, think-aloud on three. The question is completion, not satisfaction: does anyone finish all three role-plays in one sitting when told it's three?
Ongoing
Read the free-text answers against the matcher. Every answer the matcher called wrong that a human would call right is a content bug. The "I think my answer covered this" link exists to collect them.
+30 days
One question, by email: did you use any of it? This is the only row that tests the module's actual bet, and it's the weakest instrument on the page: self-report, thirty days out. Short of putting a researcher in someone's hallway, it's what I have. With a second cohort I'd replace it with an unannounced fourth role-play.
Before that
Subject-matter review. A practitioner reads the four principles for substance before any parent sees them. Plain-language paraphrase is a copyright position, not a guarantee of accuracy.

What I left for a second version

Named here rather than quietly omitted, because the reason for not building them is part of the design.

  • Tone of voice, heard rather than described. The module teaches what to say, and the same sentence lands differently said warmly or said through gritted teeth — text cannot carry that. The version I'd build is small and pull-only: three phrase cards at the close of the practice section gain a listen control, one of them offering the same words two ways with the warm read second. It sits after the practice, never during it, so a parent composing their own reply isn't invited to copy a delivery.
  • Interface sound, deliberately not. Click feedback was considered and dropped: most parents open this on a phone with the ringer off, so it never fires, and it duplicates what the visual state already says. Haptics do that job on the devices this actually runs on. Audio earns its place only where the sound is the content.
  • Assessing how the parent actually sounds. The honest limit of the practice section: it scores what was typed, not how it would have been said. Recording a spoken reply and giving feedback on delivery is a different product with a different consent conversation, and it belongs in a version that has evidence the written practice works first.

Part 1 · The three brain states model

Three brain states

How we deal with the world around us is governed, in large part, by which of three “states” our brain is in at a given moment. Each state comes with a different skillset. Conscious Discipline starts with working out which brain state you are in, followed by which state your child is in. Nothing works until we do that.

The Executive State

The higher-reasoning parts of the brain are active. Here we can think rationally, solve problems, and learn new skills.

Skillset: seeing more than one side, making reasonable choices, thinking critically about our own actions, setting long-term goals.

The Emotional State

Emotions are in control, and rational thinking is almost impossible. It draws on mental models learned through our upbringing and experience, rather than conscious thought.

Skillset: unconsciously accessing long-term memory, feeling emotions.

The Survival State

The oldest parts of the brain, working below awareness to keep us safe. Under stress, the brain drops here.

Skillset: fight, flight, or freeze (surrender). Nothing else.

Part 1 · The three brain states model

The drop

Under stress, anyone can drop a state, and it can happen fast, especially if you are at low capacity.

Capacity is your store of emotional and social energy. Yours can drain after a long day at work, or unbroken hours of childcare. Your child’s can drain just from holding it together all day at school.

The way back up

The drop can skip straight to the bottom. The rise can’t. Our brains need different things at each state in order to step their way back up.

The Executive State

Problem solving: now you’re ready to listen, think, and learn. This is where limits and new skills can land.

↑

The Emotional State

Connection: while in this state, empathy, breathing together, and naming your feelings help you connect with others and with your higher brain functions, moving you up into the Executive State.

The Survival State

Safety: until you feel physically and emotionally safe, you can’t process emotions, understand a limit or a consequence, and you can’t learn. A sense of safety allows your brain to move up and start feeling emotion.

Part 2 · Your state first

Your state first

Your child reads your state before your words reach them. So the first state to recognize is your own. Here’s how each one tends to show up in a parent.

The Executive State

A calm, composed body and voice. You can listen, see their side, and think about what comes next.

The Emotional State

Blame, lectures, sarcasm, keeping score. “You always…” Sometimes we sound like the adults who raised us.

The Survival State

A tense body and a raised voice, fists clenched, jaw tight. Words you know you’ll regret later. Or the opposite: going quiet, giving up, just wanting it over.

Part 2 · Your state first

Composure

Composure is self-regulation, and it starts with you. You need to be back in your own Executive State before you can help your child reach theirs. Without composure, nothing else lands.

It starts with one deep breath, from the diaphragm: in for a few seconds, then out for a few. It’s the quickest way to bring your reasoning back online. Your child learns these kinds of breaths at school, and practices them every day.

Part 3 · Your child’s state

Your child’s state

Reading your child’s state starts with empathy: understanding, without judgment. You’re looking for what they feel, not trying to stop it. Each state shows up in a child in its own way, and each one is asking a question. Read below to see signs and behaviors to look for to figure out your child’s state.

The Executive State

“What can I learn?”

A calm body and voice. Not necessarily a happy voice, but one ready to listen, ready to talk about what happened and hear your side.

The Emotional State

“Am I loved?”

This one shows up mostly in words. Blaming, name-calling, “you never,” denying it happened, pulling away from you.

The Survival State

“Am I safe?”

Fight: hitting, kicking, biting, screaming, tantrums. Flight: running off, hiding. Freeze: going limp, crying, giving up, or going along without taking anything in.

Coach says

Remember, your child only has so much emotional and social capacity to give at a time. Getting ready for school after a rough night’s sleep, holding it together for their teachers all day, or simply having to make lots of decisions can drain the battery. Drained, they’re much more likely to drop a state.

Part 4 · What their state needs

The Survival State: safety first

Few things are harder as a parent than keeping cool when your child is screaming, hitting, or running away. But that is exactly what your child needs when they are in the Survival State: their body is asking, “Am I safe right now?” If their body is telling them it’s not, then no pushback will land, no limits will be heard, and no rules can be taught.

We started with our state first: once we’ve figured that out, our next move is to bring ourselves into our own Executive State before we can help our child step up into theirs. Spin the wheel below to learn what you can do next.

Part 4 · What their state needs

The Emotional State: connection

She’s safe now, but now her brain is asking, “Am I loved?” She drops onto the rug, sobbing, “But these shoes feel WEIRD.”

In the Emotional State, your child may seem calmer. But now their brain is being controlled by their emotions, and their brain is asking, “Am I still loved?” Those big feelings are going to get in the way of any kind of self-reflection or lessons we want to teach. So here, they need connection. They need to feel understood, know that they are loved, and that they have a say in what happens next. Below are a couple of ways to create that connection.

Instead of asking what they feel, or why they’re upset, name it and check: “Those shoes feel wrong. It must be frustrating to think you have to wear them all day. Do I get you?”

This is a quick way to show that you empathize and aren’t judging them. There’s no such thing as a “wrong” feeling. Naming the feeling shows them that it’s okay to be feeling it.

Two positive ways out, both fine with you. They get a say in how they move forward. Depending on your child, a little mild humor might work here too: “Sneakers or boots? You pick. Or how about MY shoes? They might fit!”

Coach says

The empathy you show your child in this moment also needs to be turned back on yourself. Name your own feelings internally, and be kind to yourself.

Part 4 · What their state needs

The Executive State: now you can solve it

She sniffs, nods, says, “No way, your shoes are too big!” and picks the boots. Now she can listen and think, and now is the time to teach.

The Missing Skill

Unwelcome behavior often comes from a missing skill: a safe way to handle a problem. Teach it now, not mid-kick.

Limits for next time

Now she can learn a limit that helps next time: “Kicking hurts. When shoes feel wrong, you can say, ‘Help me.’”

Problem solving

Work it out together: “Those shoes felt weird this morning. What could we try tomorrow?”
